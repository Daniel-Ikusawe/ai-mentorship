What This Page Is For

This page explains what Vekora is, why it makes money, what makes it different from every other agency, how it grows stage by stage, and what a real client journey looks like. Read it when you need to remind yourself why you're doing this — or when you need to explain it clearly to someone else on a call.

The story that explains everything: A security firm owner is on a job fitting CCTV at a warehouse. While he's up a ladder, an enquiry comes through his website — a company wants quotes for 6 sites. He doesn't see it until 6 hours later. His competitor, who has an automated system, replied in 30 seconds. By the time the owner calls back, the company has already booked a survey with someone else. That job was worth £8,000. He lost it not because he was worse — but because he was slower.

Vekora installs a system that means the moment a lead fills in a form, three things happen automatically within 30 seconds: (1) the owner gets a text with the lead's details and a score out of 10, so they know immediately how valuable the lead is, (2) the lead gets a professional email that looks like a human wrote it specifically for them, and (3) everything gets logged in a dashboard the owner can check on their phone. No one has to do anything. It just runs.

The money argument: If this system helps a security firm close one extra job per month at £3,000 average — a job that would have gone to a competitor because they replied faster — the firm makes £3,000 more. Vekora charges £750 a month. They keep £2,250 in extra profit. The question stops being "can I afford this?" and becomes "how much am I losing every month without it?"

How to read an error message and figure out what broke — this skill transfers to everything technical you ever do.

How to write a prompt that makes an AI (like Claude) return the same structured output every single time — this is what separates people who can use AI reliably from people who get inconsistent results.

How to set up a system for someone you've never met using only a questionnaire — this is what makes you scalable to more clients.

Skills No Tutorial Teaches You But You Need Anyway

Copywriting for outreach messages. Your cold message has about 8 seconds before someone decides to ignore it. Claude writes the message but you have to brief Claude well — which means you need to understand what makes a message work. The framework is called before-after-bridge. Before: their situation right now (slow lead response, losing jobs to competitors who reply faster). After: what life looks like with the problem fixed (every lead gets a response in 30 seconds, nothing slips). Bridge: that's what Vekora does. Keep the whole message under 80 words. Practice writing briefs for Claude until your messages get 10%+ reply rates — that's your signal it's working.

Reading a Google Business Profile like a competitor. Before every prospect call, open their Google Business Profile and the top 3 competitors in their area. Look at: how many reviews each has, the average star rating, when the last post was made, and whether they reply to reviews. In 5 minutes you'll know their competitive position better than they probably do. Saying "your main competitor has 89 reviews at 4.7 stars, you have 31 at 4.1 — here's what that costs you in organic enquiries" is worth more than any pitch.

Meta Ads vocabulary. You're not running ads yet — but you will at Tier 3 and you need to understand the language so you're not caught out. Campaign = the overall goal (e.g. get leads). Ad set = who sees the ad (location, age, interests you pick). Ad = the actual image or video. CPL (cost per lead) = if you spent £100 and got 10 leads, your CPL is £10. ROAS (return on ad spend) = if you spent £100 and it generated £400 in revenue, your ROAS is 4×. Two hours on Meta Blueprint at facebook.com/business/learn covers all of this for free.

Companies House basics. Every UK company has free public records at find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk. Search any business and you can find the director's full name, when the company was started, and their SIC code (a number that says what industry they're in). This takes 10 minutes to learn and lets you open an outreach message with "Hi [director name]" and reference something specific about their business. That specificity is what makes your message feel personal instead of mass-sent.

Holding silence after you quote a price. After you say the price on a sales call, stop talking. Count to 15 in your head. It feels unbearable — like you need to fill the silence by explaining or justifying the price. Don't. The person who speaks first after a price is named is at a disadvantage. Practice this alone with a voice memo: say your price out loud, then time how long you can stay silent. Keep practicing until 15 seconds feels normal.

The Three Tools You Need To Learn

GHL — GoHighLevel
The dashboard your client sees and uses every day
What it is (plain English)
GoHighLevel (GHL) is the software your client logs into every day. It's where leads show up, where automated emails send from, where the booking calendar lives, and where the client checks how their pipeline (the list of all active leads and where each one is in the sales process) is doing. Think of it as the shop front — the client never sees n8n or Claude. They only ever see GHL. It talks to the outside world. Everything else works behind the scenes.
What it does in Vekora exactly
When a lead submits a form, GHL triggers the whole chain. It sends the lead's details to n8n via a webhook (a webhook is a way for one app to instantly notify another app that something just happened — like a text alert between two pieces of software). After n8n scores the lead using Claude, GHL fires the SMS to the owner and the personalised email to the lead. GHL also holds the calendar for bookings and the pipeline view showing all active leads with their scores.
How long to learn + where
2 weeks of evenings. Where to learn: GHL University (free — it's inside your GHL account under the help menu) and the official GHL YouTube channel. Goal for week 1: build a sub-account (a separate workspace for one client) and a pipeline from scratch. Goal for week 2: do it again from memory without googling anything. Without GHL: you cannot set up a client account, build a workflow, or send a single automated SMS.
n8n
The engine room — connects everything automatically
What it is (plain English)
n8n is a free tool that connects apps together automatically — like a set of smart pipes that moves information between GHL, Claude, and your phone without you doing anything. When a lead fills in a form, n8n decides what happens next: it sends the lead's message to Claude to get a score, fires the SMS to the owner, sends the email to the lead, and updates GHL — all in under 30 seconds. You build the flow once (called a workflow — a set of instructions that runs automatically) and it runs forever.
What it does in Vekora exactly
GHL tells n8n a lead just arrived via a webhook. n8n then: extracts the lead's fields (name, company, message, service type), sends the message to Claude's first prompt (entity extraction — pulling specific signals out of what they wrote), sends those signals plus the full lead data to Claude's second prompt (the qualifier — which scores the lead 1–10 and writes a personalised email opener), then patches (updates) all those values back into GHL as custom fields so GHL can use them in the automated SMS and email.
How long to learn + where
2 weeks of evenings after GHL. Where: n8n.io documentation and their YouTube channel — start with beginner workflow tutorials. First thing to do on Day 1: connect GHL to n8n using a webhook. Without n8n: nothing is automated. You'd be doing everything manually and your "system" would just be you checking emails.
Claude API
The AI brain — scores leads and writes personalised emails
What it is (plain English)
The Claude API is the AI brain. Inside n8n, you add one HTTP request node (a standard n8n feature that sends data to another service over the internet) that sends Claude the lead's message and asks it to: score the lead 1–10, explain why, and write a personalised first sentence for their email. Claude replies in under a second with all of that. You don't write any code at this stage — you write a clear prompt (a set of instructions telling Claude exactly what to do and what format to reply in).
What it actually does with a real lead
A lead writes "6 sites, Q2 deadline, urgent." Claude reads that and scores it 9/10, labels it hot, recommends calling today, and writes: "Hi James — managing security across six sites with a Q2 deadline is exactly where response time makes the difference." That sentence lands in their inbox in 28 seconds. Without Claude: every email opener is generic and every score is just a basic formula. Claude is what makes it feel like a human read their message and replied personally.
How long to learn + where
Half a day once you know n8n. Where: docs.anthropic.com — read the Messages API page fully. The critical skill to develop here: writing prompts that return the exact same structured output (valid JSON with specific field names) every single time, even when leads write unusual messages. This is the skill that makes the system reliable in production.

GHL itself differentiates nothing. Every agency has it. What they cannot copy requires connecting external tools to GHL in ways most no-code agencies don't know exist. These are your advantages — explained in plain English first, then in technical detail.
01
The Qualifier Is a Real Feedback Loop That Gets Smarter Every Month
Plain English: Most agencies score leads once using a fixed formula and never improve it. Yours gets smarter every month because it learns from real results — which leads actually turned into paid jobs, and which didn't.

Technical detail: Every Won and Lost deal is stored in Supabase (a free database — think of it as a spreadsheet that your n8n workflows can read and write to automatically) with the original qualifier output. A monthly n8n trigger (a scheduled task that runs automatically on a set date) pulls the last 30 closed deals, formats them as labelled examples (this lead scored 8/10 and won a £4,200 job — this lead scored 6/10 and went cold), and injects them into the Claude qualifier system prompt as few-shot examples (showing Claude real past examples so it calibrates better on future leads). The model that runs in month 6 has been trained on 6 months of real outcome data from your specific client verticals. No no-code agency can build this. This gap compounds silently and is invisible to competitors.
02
Entity Extraction From Unstructured Messages
Plain English: Most GHL agencies store the text a lead types and score it based on basic form fields (which service they selected, maybe their budget). Before your qualifier even runs, you pull specific signals out of the message itself — how many sites they mentioned, whether there's urgency language, whether they're the decision maker.

Technical detail: Before your qualifier runs, Claude does a separate entity extraction call — it reads the lead's message and pulls out: number of sites mentioned, urgency signals (deadline phrases, incident mentions, competitor comparisons), service specificity (vague vs specific), decision authority signals ("I manage" vs "my boss asked me to enquire"), budget signals. These become structured GHL custom fields automatically via the n8n HTTP PATCH request. A lead who writes "6 sites, Q2 deadline, break-in last month" gets scored completely differently to "just enquiring about prices." No native GHL workflow can parse unstructured text — it needs a real Claude API call with a structured output prompt.
03
Prospecting Research Depth No Human Can Match
Plain English: Finding 15 prospects a night, reading their reviews, checking their company registration details, and writing a personalised message for each would take a human 3–5 hours. Your agent does it in 4 minutes while you sleep.

Technical detail: A VA doing manual prospecting takes 20–30 minutes per company. Your Claude tool use agent (an AI that can use real tools like a web search or a database lookup to research companies automatically) does 15 companies in 4 minutes: reads Google reviews for recurring complaint themes (a firm with 3 reviews mentioning "slow response" is a perfect Vekora prospect), checks Companies House for ideal incorporation age, programmatically submits a test contact form and checks for auto-reply within 60 seconds, reads LinkedIn for director growth signals. The personalisation from this research is what makes your outreach convert when generic messages don't.
04
GHL Webhook Architecture Most Agencies Don't Know Exists
Plain English: Standard GHL agencies run automations entirely inside GHL — they can't bring in external data or handle errors properly. You connect GHL to n8n via webhooks (a way for one app to instantly notify another app that something just happened), which means you can do things GHL cannot do on its own.

Technical detail: You use GHL's outbound webhooks to fire every lead event to n8n in real time, enabling: multi-step conditional logic with external database queries; parallel processing (entity extraction and qualifier both run in sequence, feeding into each other — native GHL is far more limited); error handling with retry logic (if Claude API returns an error, n8n retries after 60 seconds and alerts you before the client notices). You know about system failures before your clients do. No GHL agency monitors its own execution health in real time. You do.
05
Per-Lead Email Personalisation — Not Mail Merge
Plain English: "Hi [first name]" is not personalisation — every agency does that. Before your email fires, Claude reads the lead's actual message and writes a unique opening paragraph that references what they specifically said. They think a human read their form and replied personally. It took 0.3 seconds.

Technical detail: Before your email fires, a second Claude API call runs: "Write the opening paragraph of an email to this lead. Use their message field content, company name, and specific service enquired. Reference something specific to their situation. Under 30 words." A lead who wrote "6 sites in Bristol, urgent" receives: "Hi David — managing security across six Bristol sites with a tight Q2 deadline is exactly the kind of situation where response time determines whether you get the job." That is what closes the gap between your email and their competitor's generic auto-reply.
06
Health Monitor That Detects Silent Failures

NEPQ — Jeremy Miner (Core Sales Framework)
Neuro-Emotional Persuasion Questioning. People resist being sold to, not buying. NEPQ removes the sales feeling by replacing pitches with questions. The 7-step framework:

1. Connect — no pitch, genuine curiosity.
2. Situation questions — "Walk me through what happens when a new enquiry comes in right now." Let them talk fully without interrupting.
3. Problem questions — surface the pain, don't invent it. "How long does that usually take?" "What happens at 9pm on a Friday?"
4. Consequence questions — "Has a job ever gone to a competitor because they responded faster?" "How often?" "What's a job like that worth?"
5. Solution awareness — "If there were a way to have every enquiry responded to in 30 seconds automatically, with a score telling you who to call first — would that change anything?" Only ask this AFTER they've named the pain themselves twice.
6. Demo — only after they've confirmed the pain. The demo answers the question they just said yes to.
7. Assumptive close — "Based on what you told me — [their exact words] — does what you just saw solve that?" Then silence. Every word you add after asking weakens the close.

The 5 Rules of Effective Prompts
Master these before anything else
01
Rule 1 — Give Claude a role, context, and constraints all at once
Bad: "Write an outreach message."
Good: "You are writing a cold WhatsApp message on behalf of Vekora, an AI automation agency targeting independent security installers in the UK with 3–15 employees. The recipient is a director named [Name] at [Company]. The goal is to get a reply — not to sell anything. The message must be under 80 words, open with a genuine question (not a statement about what Vekora does), and feel like it was written by a real person who did 2 minutes of research on their business, not a mass-sent template."

The second prompt produces a useable message. The first produces something generic. The difference: role (writing for Vekora), context (who the recipient is), constraints (under 80 words, question not statement, not template-like). All three together are what produce reliable output.
Rule 2 — Specify the exact output format for structured data
For anything you're going to parse programmatically (like the Vekora qualifier): "Return ONLY a valid JSON object with these exact fields. No explanation before or after. No markdown code blocks. No preamble. Just the raw JSON object starting with { and ending with }:
{ "priority_score": integer 1–10, "tier": "hot" or "warm" or "cold", "recommended_action": "call_today" or "email_first" or "nurture" or "pass", "email_opener": "string under 30 words", "estimated_value": integer in GBP }"

The instruction "no markdown code blocks" is critical — by default Claude wraps code in ```json ... ``` which breaks your JSON parser. "Just the raw JSON" eliminates that. The instruction "no explanation before or after" eliminates preamble text that would also break parsing. Test this with 20 different inputs including weird edge cases before trusting it in production.
Rule 3 — Few-shot prompting: show examples of what you want
Few-shot prompting means giving Claude 2–3 examples of exactly what you want before asking it to do the real task. "Here are two examples of the kind of email opener I want Claude to write. Example 1: Lead wrote 'CCTV for 3 sites by Q2' → Opener: 'Hi David — coordinating security across three sites ahead of a Q2 deadline is exactly the kind of situation where a 6-hour response time costs real jobs.' Example 2: [another example]. Now write an opener for this lead: [new lead]."

Claude calibrates to your examples and produces output that matches the style, length, and tone you've shown — much more consistently than if you just describe what you want in words. Few-shot is especially powerful for tone-sensitive tasks like cold outreach where the vibe matters as much as the words.
Rule 4 — Chain of thought for complex reasoning
For tasks that require multiple logical steps (scoring a lead, planning a strategy, analysing a situation), add: "Think through this step by step before giving your final answer." This single instruction significantly improves output quality on complex tasks because it forces the model to reason sequentially rather than jumping to a pattern-matched answer. For structured JSON output where you only need the final result: don't use this instruction — it adds verbose reasoning text before the JSON that breaks your parser. Chain of thought is for human-readable complex reasoning, not machine-parsed structured output.
Rule 5 — Test with edge cases before trusting in production
A prompt that works on normal inputs might break on edge cases. For the Vekora qualifier, test with: blank message field (lead submitted form with nothing in the message), message in Polish or Spanish, message that's only emojis, message that's 5,000 characters long, message that contains SQL injection characters ('; DROP TABLE contacts; --), message from a robot (test form submissions often look like spam). Fix every break. A prompt that works on 95% of inputs is not production-ready — that's 1 in 20 leads getting a broken response. Fix every edge case you can think of, then test for 30 days in production before calling it done.

Advanced Prompting Techniques
After mastering the 5 rules
02
System prompts vs user prompts — know the difference
When calling the API directly (as in Vekora's qualifier), every API call has two sections: the system prompt and the user message. The system prompt is the persistent set of instructions that applies to every message Claude receives in this context — it's Claude's "job description" for this task. The user message is the specific data for this particular request. Best practice: put all context, role, format requirements, and constraints in the system prompt. Put only the variable data (the actual lead fields) in the user message. This way the instructions never accidentally get mixed up with the data, and the output format is consistent every single call.
XML tags for structured prompts
For complex prompts with multiple sections, use XML tags to separate them clearly. Claude is trained to respond well to XML structure. Example:
<context>You are Vekora's qualifier AI for UK GOS businesses.</context>
<lead_data>Name: James Carter. Company: ShieldSafe. Message: 6 sites, Q2.</lead_data>
<entity_signals>sites_mentioned: 6, urgency: true, deadline: Q2.</entity_signals>
<instructions>Score this lead. Return only JSON.</instructions>

XML tags make it clear to Claude which part of the prompt is context, which is data, and which is instructions. This reduces ambiguity and improves consistency in complex prompts with many moving parts.
Temperature control for consistency vs creativity
Temperature is a setting that controls how random or predictable Claude's output is. Temperature 0 = completely deterministic — given the same input, Claude returns the same output every single time. Temperature 1 = more creative and varied — same input might produce slightly different outputs each run. For Vekora's qualifier (which must return consistent, parseable JSON every single time): set temperature to 0 explicitly in your API call. For marketing copy, outreach message generation, or anything where variety is good: use 0.7–1. Default API temperature is ~1. If you're seeing inconsistent output from a production system, temperature 0 is often the fix.
Building with AI means writing code that calls AI models programmatically — so the AI becomes a component inside your systems, not just a chat interface you type into manually. This is what makes Vekora's qualifier work as a live production system, and it's the core technical skill behind every AI-powered product you'll build in the future.

How the API works — plain English
API stands for Application Programming Interface — it's a way for your code to talk to another service over the internet, like a phone line between your Python script and Anthropic's servers. You send a message and get a reply. In practice: you install the anthropic Python library (pip install anthropic), create a client object using your API key (a secret password that proves to Anthropic you're allowed to use the service), call client.messages.create() with the model name and a list of messages, and read Claude's reply from the response object. Retry logic — critical for production systems
The Anthropic API can occasionally return a rate limit error (too many requests at once) or a server error (something went wrong on Anthropic's side). Without retry logic, your qualifier crashes when this happens and the lead gets no response. With retry logic: if the API call fails, wait a short time (exponential backoff — wait 1 second after the first failure, 2 seconds after the second, 4 after the third) and try again. Also: Claude occasionally returns malformed JSON — wraps it in markdown code blocks or adds a sentence before it. Your retry: catch the JSONDecodeError, prefix the prompt with "Return ONLY the raw JSON object, nothing else:" and try once more. Two retries handle 99% of real-world failures.
Never hardcode your API key — ever
Your API key is a password that costs real money when used. Never write it directly in your code. If you push code to GitHub with a hardcoded API key, the key is visible to everyone and Anthropic will automatically revoke it. Instead: create a file called .env in your project folder (this is a plain text file that stores secrets). Write ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=your-actual-key-here inside it. Add .env to your .gitignore file (this tells Git to never include it in commits). In your Python code, use python-dotenv to load it: load_dotenv() then os.environ.get("ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"). Every credential you ever use in code — API keys, database passwords, Stripe secret keys — lives in .env, never in the code itself.
Level 2 — Building AI Agents With Tool Use
After Level 1 · Used in Projects P6 and P8

What an AI agent actually is — the ReAct loop
An agent is a loop. Instead of calling Claude once and getting a final answer, you call Claude repeatedly. Each time Claude can either: (a) use a tool to get more information — like searching the web, looking up a company in a database, or testing a contact form, or (b) produce a final answer when it has enough information. You keep the loop running until Claude stops requesting tools and returns the end result.

This is called the ReAct pattern (Reason and Act). Claude reasons about what it needs to know, then acts by calling a tool, then reasons again with the new information, then acts again if needed. The loop is: messages.create() with tools → check if response.stop_reason is "tool_use" → if yes: execute the tool functions and send results back → loop again → if stop_reason is "end_turn": extract and return the final text.

This is how Project P6 (the prospecting agent) works: Claude reasons it needs to know about the company's reviews, calls the search_company tool, reads the results, reasons it also needs the director's name, calls the get_director tool, reads that, and finally produces a personalised outreach message using all the information it gathered. You don't tell Claude when to call which tool — Claude decides based on what it needs.
import anthropic client = anthropic.Anthropic() # Define what tools Claude can use — Claude reads these descriptions to decide when to call each tool tools = [ { "name": "search_company", "description": "Search for a company's online reviews and star rating. Critical rules for building agents that work reliably
Tool description field is the most important part. This is what Claude reads to decide whether to call the tool and when. A vague description ("gets company info") causes Claude to call the tool at wrong times or miss obvious cases where it should have used it. A precise description ("searches for a company's Google reviews and star rating — use when you need to know whether a company has customer complaints about slow response times") tells Claude exactly what the tool does and when it's relevant.

Handle multiple tool calls in one response. Claude can request multiple tools in a single response. Your code must iterate over all blocks in response.content, find every tool_use block, execute all of them, and send all results back in one user message. If you only handle the first tool and miss the others, Claude's next reasoning step will be based on incomplete information.

Set a max turns limit. Without it, a misbehaving agent can loop indefinitely, burning API credits and never finishing. 10 turns is a reasonable limit for most research agents.

Add human-in-the-loop checkpoints before irreversible actions. If your agent is going to send an email, write to a database, or make any external API call that costs money or can't be undone — pause and ask for confirmation before executing. During development: log what the agent is about to do and require a manual "yes" before it proceeds. In production: implement an approval mechanism for high-stakes actions.
Level 3 — RAG Systems and AI Apps
Imperial Year 1 onwards · Used in Project P8
03
RAG — Retrieval Augmented Generation (plain English + technical)
Plain English: RAG is how you give Claude access to a large private knowledge base — case studies, client data, company documents — that doesn't fit in a single prompt. Instead of pasting everything every time, you store it as embeddings (a mathematical representation of meaning — converting text into a list of numbers that captures what it's about, not just the words it uses) and at query time you find the most relevant pieces and include only those.

How it works technically:
1. Chunking: split your documents into small pieces (300–500 words each).
2. Embedding: send each chunk to an embedding model (text-embedding-3-small from OpenAI, or Anthropic's future embedding API) which converts it to a vector — a list of ~1536 numbers representing its meaning.
3. Storage: store each vector in a vector database (Pinecone, Supabase pgvector) alongside the original text it came from.
4. Query: when a new enquiry comes in, embed the query with the same model to get its vector. Search the vector database for the top 3 most similar vectors using cosine similarity (a mathematical measure of how close two vectors are — close = similar meaning). Retrieve the original text chunks those vectors came from.
5. Injection: put those 3 chunks into Claude's prompt as context. "Here are 3 similar past cases: [chunk 1] [chunk 2] [chunk 3]. Now answer this new enquiry using these as reference."

Why it matters for Vekora: Project P8's proposal generator uses RAG to match a new prospect enquiry against your 5+ Vekora case studies and generate a personalised proposal referencing real matched results. That personalisation is what closes GOS clients.
Fine-tuning vs prompting — when to use each
Fine-tuning means training an existing model on your specific data so it bakes in your patterns permanently — after fine-tuning, you don't need to provide examples in the prompt because the model already knows them. Prompting means giving Claude context and instructions each time you call it.

For 99% of applications including everything you'll build in the next 2 years: prompting + RAG is better than fine-tuning. Here's why: fine-tuning is expensive (costs hundreds to thousands of pounds per training run), slow to iterate (if you want to change behaviour you have to retrain), requires thousands of high-quality examples to work well, and often produces worse results than good prompting on tasks that require reasoning. Fine-tuning is best for: very narrow, repetitive tasks with thousands of consistent examples (like a customer service bot that always answers the same 20 questions in the same format). For everything else — use prompting with RAG. The accuracy of prompting + RAG today is good enough that fine-tuning adds minimal benefit for most tasks.

How Language Models Actually Work
Understand before building anything serious
01
What Claude is actually doing when it responds
Claude is a large language model (LLM). It was trained on an enormous amount of text — books, websites, code, research papers, conversations. During training, it learned to predict: "given this sequence of text, what word (technically: what token — a piece of a word, roughly 0.75 words on average) is most likely to come next?" That task sounds simple but predicting the next token accurately across all types of text requires the model to learn vast amounts of knowledge, reasoning ability, and language structure. The result is a model that can answer questions, write code, analyse arguments, and reason about complex situations — not because it "knows things" like a database lookup, but because it has learned deep patterns of what good, coherent, accurate text looks like.
Why Claude makes mistakes — hallucination explained
Claude generates the most likely next token based on patterns — it doesn't verify facts against a real-world database or look things up. If the correct answer to a question is a specific phone number, address, or statistic, Claude may generate a plausible-sounding number rather than the correct one. This is called hallucination — the model is confidently wrong. It happens because the model has learned what plausible answers look like, not what the actual correct answer is.

How to reduce hallucination in your systems: Constrain the output space (asking for a 1–10 score with specific criteria is much less likely to hallucinate than asking for an open-ended analysis). Provide the source data directly in the prompt (if you give Claude the lead's actual message, it can't hallucinate what they said). Use temperature 0 for factual tasks. Add a verification step for critical outputs. Always verify specific facts (phone numbers, addresses, statistics) from Claude's output against a real source before using them.
Tokens, context windows, and why they matter practically
A token is roughly 0.75 words. "Hello world" = 2 tokens. A 1,000-word email = ~1,300 tokens. A token is the unit Claude thinks in — you don't see tokens when using Claude.ai but they matter when calling the API because you're billed per token and each model has a context window limit.

Context window = the maximum number of tokens Claude can "see" in one conversation — both the input (everything you've sent) and the output (everything Claude has replied) combined. Claude Sonnet 4's context window is 200,000 tokens (~150,000 words — about 600 pages of text). For most tasks this is irrelevant. It matters when building RAG systems: your document chunks + Claude's prompt + Claude's response must all fit within the context window. If they don't fit, Claude can't see the whole context and its responses degrade.
What's Actually Inside a Model
Good to understand before Imperial's ML modules
02
Neural networks in 4 sentences
A neural network is a mathematical system made up of layers of numbers (called weights or parameters) that transform an input into an output. During training, the weights are adjusted millions or billions of times using an algorithm called backpropagation (which calculates how to adjust each weight to reduce the difference between the model's output and the correct answer). After training, the weights are frozen and the network can process new inputs it's never seen before. Every AI model — image classifiers, language models, voice recognition — is a neural network of some kind at its core.
The transformer architecture — why it changed everything
Before 2017, the best language models processed text word by word in sequence (like reading left to right) — slow to train and hard to scale to long documents. The transformer (introduced in the paper "Attention Is All You Need" by researchers at Google in 2017) processes all words simultaneously and uses a mechanism called attention to learn which words in a sequence are most relevant to each other. "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired" — to understand what "it" refers to, you need to connect "it" back to "cat" many words earlier. Attention learns these long-range connections automatically. This made models much faster to train (all positions processed in parallel), scalable to enormous datasets, and significantly better at understanding meaning across long text. GPT, Claude, Gemini — every major LLM today is built on the transformer architecture.
RLHF — how Claude was trained to be helpful
RLHF stands for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. After a base language model is trained on text, it can generate plausible text but it doesn't naturally try to be helpful, harmless, or honest — it just predicts what comes next. RLHF is the extra training step that shapes the model's behaviour. Human raters compare pairs of model responses and say which one is better. This preference data is used to train a "reward model" (a model that predicts which responses humans prefer). Then the language model is further trained using reinforcement learning (a training technique where the model is rewarded for actions that produce high reward model scores and penalised for actions that produce low scores) to generate responses that humans prefer. Claude was trained using Constitutional AI (Anthropic's specific version of RLHF) — a process that includes a set of principles Claude uses to evaluate and improve its own outputs.

The 5-Step Interview Framework — Every Time
1
Clarify before writing a single line
"Can the array contain duplicate values? What's the maximum input size I should expect? Should I handle a null input or can I assume the input is always valid?" Ask 2–3 clarifying questions before touching the keyboard. This shows systematic thinking and prevents you from solving the wrong version of the problem. Silent coders who immediately start typing look junior regardless of how fast they code.
2
State your approach out loud before coding
"I'm thinking I'll use a hash map to track character frequencies in O(n) time and O(n) space — does that approach work for this problem?" Get the interviewer's agreement on the approach before writing a single line of code. This prevents wasting 15 minutes implementing the wrong approach, shows structured thinking, and opens a conversation where the interviewer can guide you toward the optimal solution if yours is suboptimal. This is called "thinking out loud" and it's the single most important interview skill to develop.
3
Write clean code while narrating
Clear variable names (not x, i, j everywhere — use left, right, frequency_map). Comments on non-obvious logic. Talk through every line: "Here I'm initialising the hash map because I'll need O(1) lookup in the inner loop — storing each character as the key and its count as the value." The talking is as important as the code. Interviewers are evaluating whether they'd want to work alongside you. A developer who explains their thinking clearly while coding is someone you can collaborate with.
4
Test edge cases before saying you're done
Walk through at least 2 test cases on the whiteboard: a normal case and at least one edge case (empty input, single element, all duplicates, maximum size, negative numbers). Find your own bugs before the interviewer finds them for you. Catching your own mistake and fixing it calmly is a strong positive signal — it shows you test your work, not just that you made a mistake.
5
State time and space complexity unprompted
"The time complexity is O(n) because we iterate through the array once, and the space complexity is O(n) in the worst case because the hash map could contain every unique element." Say this before they ask. If the optimal solution has better complexity, acknowledge it: "There's likely an O(1) space solution using two pointers instead — I went with the hash map approach first for clarity. Want me to optimise?" This shows you understand complexity, you're aware there may be a better solution, and you're thinking beyond just making it work.

CLAUSE X — Third-Party API and Infrastructure Cost Pass-Through

X.1  Vekora's monthly retainer fee covers standard third-party API and infrastructure usage up to the Fair Use Threshold applicable to the Client's service tier, as set out in Schedule 1 (the "Threshold"). All usage below the Threshold is included within the retainer at no additional charge.

X.2  Where actual third-party costs attributable to the Client's usage exceed the Threshold in any calendar month ("Excess Costs"), Vekora shall notify the Client in writing within 5 business days of identifying the overage. Notification shall include a usage breakdown itemising the source and approximate volume of excess usage by service type (e.g. AI inference, cloud hosting, communication services).

X.3  Excess Costs shall be invoiced to the Client at cost with no markup added by Vekora. Invoices for Excess Costs are payable within 14 days of the invoice date.

X.4  Vekora shall take reasonable steps to optimise third-party API usage on an ongoing basis and shall not permit costs attributable to the Client to exceed twice the Threshold (the "Hard Cap") in any calendar month without the Client's prior written approval.

X.5  On request, Vekora shall provide the Client with a monthly API usage summary showing aggregate usage by service type. Detailed raw logs shall be retained by Vekora for a minimum of 90 days and made available to the Client on request within 5 business days.

X.6  Third-party costs for the purposes of this clause include but are not limited to: AI inference fees (including fees charged by Anthropic for use of the Claude API), cloud hosting and compute costs (including Railway, Supabase, and Vercel), voice AI services (including ElevenLabs), and communication services (including Twilio SMS and email infrastructure).

SCHEDULE 1 — Fair Use Thresholds by Service Tier

Tier 1 (£750/month): Threshold £10/month · Hard Cap £25/month
Tier 2 (£1,200/month): Threshold £15/month · Hard Cap £35/month
Tier 3 (£1,700/month): Threshold £20/month · Hard Cap £50/month

Why Each Part of the Clause Exists
"At cost, no markup": Removes any financial incentive you could have to let usage run high. Clients read this and feel protected. Marked-up overages imply you're profiting from their usage — that creates suspicion. Zero markup + transparency = zero friction.

5-day notification window: You must tell them before you bill them. A client who gets a surprise invoice without prior warning is a client who churns. A client who gets a message saying "your team's usage of the pricing AI exceeded the threshold this month — here's the breakdown, I'll send a £38 invoice" stays, because you communicated early and clearly.

Hard Cap requiring written approval: This protects the client from runaway costs and you from a dispute about a £400 invoice. Nothing above 2× the threshold without them approving it. In practice the hard cap will almost never be relevant — but its presence signals you are not trying to exploit the clause.

Schedule 1 (the threshold table): Thresholds are set well above your realistic per-client costs at every tier. Tier 1 real cost is £0.31/month — the threshold is £10. The clause almost never triggers at Tier 1–3. This is intentional: the clause should feel like a safety net to the client, not a regular line item on their invoice.

Sub-processors listed in X.6: UK GDPR requires you to name sub-processors in your DPA. Listing them in the cost clause too makes the document internally consistent and demonstrates you know your own stack. Clients appreciate specificity.
